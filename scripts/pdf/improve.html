How a tool earns its score.

Every tool in the directory is scored from 0 to 5 on six questions that come from Ivan Illich's book Tools for Conviviality, for a total out of 30. This guide shows what each question looks at and what you can change, in roughly the order it is worth doing.

01 / FOOTPRINTDoes it run light?Works offline and on old hardware, with nothing running that the job doesn't need.
02 / LOCK-INCan people leave with their stuff?Open formats, full export, no account you don't need.
03 / SELF-DIRECTIONCan a person bend it to their purposes?Learnable by using it, with no dark patterns training their habits.
04 / POWERWho holds the controls?Source, governance, fair pricing, and whether your data is the product.
05 / LONGEVITYDoes it outlive its maker?Keeps working if the company folds or the person stops paying.
06 / DELIVERYDoes it do what it promises?No ads, no engagement bait, no promise the product can't keep.

How to use this guide

Read the list on the next page, pick the changes that suit your project, and make them in small steps. Each question then has a page of its own with what a reviewer looks at, what a low, middling and high score looks like, and the evidence worth putting where reviewers can find it. A page on money follows, because it is the first thing most makers worry about, and the guide ends with how to submit.

A score is a judgement. A reviewer scores your tool independently, dated and open to change, and nothing in this guide guarantees a number. It also costs nothing: the directory takes no payment for listings or reviews.

Tools for ConvivialityQuestion 1 of 6
01
From “Biological degradation”

Footprint

Does it run light, work offline, and keep going on an old laptop, or does it need new hardware and a data centre?

What a reviewer looks at

  • Whether the core job works with no connection.
  • How heavy it is: install size or page weight, number of dependencies, background processes.
  • The oldest device or browser it supports.
  • Telemetry and trackers that run for the maker's benefit.
  • AI features that could be optional or local but are required and remote.

What the scores look like

1Needs a constant connection and a recent device, with heavy work running in the background.
3Runs on common hardware, needs the network for most tasks, carries a few dependencies it doesn't need.
5Runs light on old hardware, the core tasks work offline, and nothing runs that the job doesn't need.

This week

  • Remove unused dependencies and any tracking scripts.
  • Lazy-load heavy features, and make AI features optional.
  • Test on the oldest device you can find and write down what happened.

This month

  • Cache what people read so it opens with no connection.
  • Keep data in local storage and sync only on request.
  • Replace background polling with something that waits to be asked.

Evidence worth showing

A line in the README with the minimum requirements and the oldest device you tested, the size of the install or page, and a list of which features need the network.

Tools for ConvivialityQuestion 2 of 6
02
From “Radical monopoly”

Lock-in

Can you leave with your stuff in an open format, or does it make you depend on a whole category of product to take part?

What a reviewer looks at

  • Export and import, and whether the export holds everything or only a slice.
  • Whether files are open formats that other tools read.
  • Whether an account is needed to see your own data.
  • A documented API or data schema.
  • Whether anything stops working for people who leave.

What the scores look like

1Data lives only inside the app, with no export, and an account is needed to see it.
3Export exists but is lossy, buried or limited to one format.
5Full export and import in open formats, local use without an account, a documented API.

This week

  • Add one export button that saves everything, not a subset, as JSON, CSV or Markdown.
  • Publish the data schema in the README.
  • Write down how someone moves to another tool.

This month

  • Let people use it with no account when no server is needed.
  • Import from the formats your competitors use.
  • Keep export working after a subscription ends.

Evidence worth showing

A link to the export documentation, a sample exported file in the repository, and a screenshot of the export menu.

A good test: can someone leave in ten minutes, taking everything, without asking you for anything? That is the standard a five describes.

Tools for ConvivialityQuestion 3 of 6
03
From “Overprogramming”

Self-direction

Can a normal person learn it by using it and point it at their own purposes, without dark patterns training their habits?

What a reviewer looks at

  • How quickly a new person gets a result, without a tour or a sign-up wall.
  • Sensible defaults, undo, and settings that change what the tool does.
  • Room to adapt it: templates, shortcuts, plugins, scripting.
  • Accessibility.
  • Patterns that exist to bring people back rather than to help them: streaks, badges, confetti, nag modals, fake urgency, hidden cancel buttons.

What the scores look like

1Pushes people along a fixed path with nags and streaks, and makes leaving or cancelling hard.
3Clear and decent, with good defaults, but little room to bend it.
5Learnable in minutes, with undo, settings and shortcuts, no manipulation, and room for people to make it theirs.

This week

  • Delete streaks, badges and confetti that exist to bring people back.
  • Remove nag modals and fake countdowns.
  • Make cancelling as easy as signing up.

This month

  • Add undo wherever something can be lost.
  • Add keyboard shortcuts and a way to change defaults.
  • Open one door to adaptation, such as templates or plugins.
  • Run a basic accessibility pass.

Evidence worth showing

A short note on your design principles, a list of the settings people can change, and screenshots of the first run and the cancel flow.

Tools for ConvivialityQuestion 4 of 6
04
From “Polarization”

Power

Who holds the controls? Team size, governance, open source, whether the price shuts people out, and whether your data is the product.

What a reviewer looks at

  • The licence and whether the source is public.
  • Who decides, and how a person can reach them.
  • How the tool is paid for, and whether that depends on people's data or attention.
  • Pricing, and whether it shuts people out of what they need.
  • Telemetry, data sharing and self-hosting.

What the scores look like

1Closed, paid for by ads or data, one company decides everything and changes the terms at will.
3Closed or company-controlled, with a fair price and limited data collection.
5Open source under a recognised licence, collects only what it needs, funding is public, and decisions sit with a foundation, co-op or community.

This week

  • Add a LICENSE file, if you own the rights and agree.
  • Remove third-party trackers.
  • Write who runs the project and how it is paid for.

This month

  • Write a plain pricing page with a free or low-cost way in.
  • Offer self-hosting.
  • Add a way for users to take part in decisions: an open tracker, a forum or a roadmap.

Evidence worth showing

The LICENSE, a funding statement, a privacy note, and a pricing page that a stranger can follow.

Power is where money questions meet the score. The next page deals with them directly.

Tools for ConvivialityQuestion 5 of 6
05
From “Obsolescence”

Longevity

Does it keep working if the company folds or you stop paying, and does it respect the hardware you already own?

What a reviewer looks at

  • Whether it works if the maker's server disappears.
  • Standard formats, and a file format that is documented.
  • A self-hosting guide, or a local mode.
  • A licence that lets others fork it.
  • Dependencies, forced upgrades and support for older devices.
  • What happens to a person's data when they stop paying.

What the scores look like

1Stops working if the server goes away or a subscription lapses, uses a private format, forces updates.
3Data is portable and the tool is maintained, but core work needs the maker's server.
5Works with no server or has a documented way to host it, uses open formats, old versions keep working, and a plan exists for what happens if the maker stops.

This week

  • Write an “If we stop” section in the README: what happens to the service and to people's data.
  • Pin dependencies and note the oldest supported device.
  • Document the file format.

This month

  • Ship a local-only mode, or a self-hosting guide.
  • Keep old file versions readable.
  • Give lapsed subscribers read-only access to their own data.

Evidence worth showing

The “If we stop” section, the self-hosting guide, a format description, and the date of your last release.

Tools for ConvivialityQuestion 6 of 6
06
From “Frustration”

Delivery

Does it actually do the job, without ads, engagement bait, or a promise the product can't keep?

What a reviewer looks at

  • Whether the core task works end to end.
  • Whether the README and website describe what the tool really does.
  • Whether limits are stated and errors say what to do next.
  • Ads, upsell prompts and engagement tricks that sit in the way of the job.

What the scores look like

1Fails at its main task or promises much more than it delivers, with ads or upsells in the way.
3Does the job with rough edges and some upsell.
5Does the core job reliably, says what it can't do, shows no ads, and has errors that help.

This week

  • Write a Limitations section in the README.
  • Rewrite marketing copy so every claim is true today.
  • Fix the three most common failures in the main task.

This month

  • Rewrite error messages to say what happened and what to do.
  • Remove ads and upsells from the core path.
  • Add tests for the main task and a changelog people can read.

Evidence worth showing

A Limitations list, a changelog, a short demo or walkthrough, and claims that match the product.

Delivery is the question the big companies tend to win. A small tool can match them here by doing one job reliably and being honest about the rest.

Tools for ConvivialityMoney

What conviviality does to your income.

Less than you might fear, in either direction. The score measures how a tool treats the people who use it, and it does not measure how the tool earns a living, so a tool can charge money and still score high.

Some examples from the directory. Cryptomator charges about 30 euros to edit files on a phone and scores 27. Bitwarden has paid plans and scores 25. Obsidian sells sync and publishing and scores 24, Ente sells storage and scores 24, and Kagi is a paid search engine with no ads that scores 21. What the score looks at is how you earn: whether you charge for the service you provide, or for the way out of it.

What each source of income does to the score

Where the money comes fromEffect
A one-time purchaseNo cost to the score. It often helps Longevity.
A subscription for hosting, sync or storageNo cost, if the tool still works without it and export stays open.
Paid support, training or custom workNo cost.
Donations, sponsors and grantsNo cost. Saying openly who funds you helps Power.
AdvertisingCosts Power and Delivery.
Selling or sharing user dataCosts Power, and badly.
Charging to export, or holding data after someone cancelsCosts Lock-in and Longevity.
Trial traps and hard-to-cancel plansCosts Self-direction.
Charge for the service, not for the exit.

The honest limits

We have no evidence that a higher score earns more, and we don't promise it. Making a tool more convivial can remove income that came from lock-in or from selling data, and you should count that loss against the gain. It can also lower your costs, with fewer servers and less support, and earn trust that is hard to buy. Work it out with your own numbers: list each way you earn money, mark the ones that depend on keeping people in, and decide what you are willing to change.

1

3
Fill in the form at toolsforconviviality.org/suggest/. It prepares a message for you to check and send to hello@toolsforconviviality.org. It sends nothing by itself.
